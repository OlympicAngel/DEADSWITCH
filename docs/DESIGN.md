# DEADSWITCH: design

You are what is left of the war AI that ended the world. Rebuild a base in the ruins, arm it, conquer the wasteland, and find out who you were. Your choices decide what you become.

## Core loop
1. **Economy**: Scrip (money), Energy, Population. Energy and Population have storage caps (Battery Bank, Habitat Block). Buildings level up one at a time through a timed build queue.
   - *Producers* make a resource for free. *Converters* turn one resource into another at a loss (output worth = input worth x 0.6, with worth Scrip 1, Energy 1, Population 12; `CONVERSION` in data), so no chain creates value. Production bonuses speed converters up on both sides without improving the ratio. They throttle themselves on empty input or full output, and can be switched off.
   - The **AI Core** caps every other building at `core level × 5` and opens new buildings and new chapters. It can only be upgraded once the average level of all unlocked production and storage buildings (unbuilt count as 0; converters, the Watch Daemon and military and research facilities do not count) reaches 50% of the current cap.
   - **Opening pace**: while the AI Core is still level 1 every build takes a third of its time (`BALANCE.earlyBuildSpeed`), so the first half hour is spent deciding rather than waiting.
   - **Building prices and times**: every building's Scrip price carries `buildingPriceMult` (x1.5) on top of the global `priceMult`, and every build runs `buildTimeMult` (x1.5) longer with a further `(n + 1)^buildTimePower` climb per level, the same shape as the price curve. A build set to run longer than `BALANCE.longBuild` (15 minutes) is worth a notification when it lands, so a long one is something to walk away from rather than sit through.
   - **Time away**: the world runs for up to `warHours` (8) of any absence whether or not anything was built for it; the **Watch Daemon** (Economy, Storage tab) decides only how long the *base keeps producing* and at what share. Without one, nothing is produced past the first two minutes, so you come back to what the wasteland did rather than to a stopped clock: the report lists the attacks, the ground and the orders, never resources, and names the Daemon. Nothing is shown at all if the world did nothing. The Daemon's first level pays for 1 hour at 50% of normal output, then levels alternate: odd levels add an hour, even levels add 5% efficiency up to 80%, after which every level adds time (`BALANCE.offline`). An absence brings at most `attacksPerHour` x the hours it ran, capped at 4, and orders by tier (`EVENTS_CFG.offlineTiers`): one from two hours, two from five, three from eight. A crisis out of a breach spends that same budget, or a night of breaches queues a pile of damage reports that all open the moment the player gets back. The report counts what is still waiting as well as what was answered, so the orders that open behind it are not a surprise. The window covers the whole absence, also when a background tab catches up in chunks, and nothing past it is ever worth a notification.
   - *Unlockers* (Barracks, Armory, Fortification Works, Think Tank, Research Lab) open Arsenal tabs; each level unlocks more and cuts that tab's prices 3%.
2. **Arsenal**: unlimited units at escalating prices. Every building level and unit costs `base × priceMult × g^n × (n+1)^pricePower` with `g = 1 + (growth-1) × growthMult × resourceGrowth` (1.8, 1.35, 0.35; Scrip climbs at 0.8 of the rate) raise the three factors:
   - Some advanced units have **durability** (up to 70%, from about the third unit of a tab on, uneven by role: expendable drones and gun trucks have none, artillery, emplacements and remote drone operators have a lot): each type loses `share × (1 − durability)` of its units in a defeat, rounded; a defeat always costs at least one unit (the least durable type).
   - **Coming back**: one absence is one modal. Every battle the catch-up resolved is already counted in the report, so the individual attack reports it queued are dropped rather than stacked behind it; cut scenes stay. The report opens before anything else the absence queued, and looking in twice without reading it adds the second stretch into the first instead of queueing a second report.
   - A converter at a standstill does nothing rather than dividing by it. The unpaid part of an absence runs at speed 0, and asking how much of nothing fits in a full store is 0/0: that answered NaN and spread through every stockpile, which is how a save could come back infinite. Nothing that is not a finite number ever reaches `s.res` now (the tick is rolled back for that resource), a save carrying one heals on load, and a save too far gone falls back to the session it grew out of without the player having to find the restore button.
   - Production stops at the brim of the stores, but a reward never evaporates for want of a shelf: capture loot and what an order pays overfill (`grant`), the pill reads **OVER n**, and the overflow drains as it is spent rather than on the next tick.
   - **AI Power** wins operations. **AI Defense** holds off raids. **AI Experts** add +0.5% to all production each. **Tech** adds % bonuses.
   - **Troops** (Military Staff) are the only units that count for both Power and Defense, and the only ones paid for in people. Per scrip spent they beat either specialist tab, because they come with a condition: while an operation is running, nothing in that tab holds the wall. The Defense hex on Command reads `Defense −N` in amber, the inbound-attack row is tagged **troops out**, the Outgoing panel says what marched with it, and the Troops tab names the hole. Taking ground and holding it at the same time is the one thing the arsenal cannot do.
3. **Operations** (map): attack sectors adjacent to territory you hold. Operations hit with AI Power x `OPS.powerMult` (2), and **Threat does not see that multiplier**, so pressing harder on the map never calls down a heavier war on the Nest. Odds are `P^4 / (P^4 + D^4)` (equal = 50%, double = 94%), shown before you launch and rolled when the operation lands. Wins give a permanent % bonus while the sector is held — Scavenger ground pays in resources only, from 0.5% at Rust Market to 5% at the Scrap Throne, scaled by what the sector is worth, so the first clan's map is an economy rather than an arsenal — plus fixed loot and a memory fragment the first time (retaking a lost sector restores the bonus only; `OPS.loot` sizes loot from the sector's base defense; capitals x2.5). A failed operation kills 11% of Military Staff and destroys 6.5% of weapons × (their defense / your power), capped at 60% and 45% (caps not shown), so a losing attack costs an army, not a rounding error; a win costs less (2.5% and 1.2%) and only when the odds were close enough for those shares to round up to whole units, while a defeat always costs at least one. The report names what the dead were worth in AI Power or Defense, and what the other side lost: a captured sector's whole defense, or 6% of it when they repel you (on top of the +16% a repelled operation gives them). Capturing a faction's capital stops its raids. **Clan support**: a sector gets +50% defense while every sector of its own faction it links to still stands, falling linearly to 0 as you take them (`OPS.flankBonus`); links to other factions give nothing.
   - **The map** is an open world you drag around and pinch (or wheel, or the +/− buttons) to zoom between 50% and 150%: 47 sectors in a web around the Nest (`tools/map-layout.mjs` generates positions and non-crossing links; most sectors have 2-3 links, some 1 or 4; the capitals are the cities, with 5 links mostly to their own clan). Factions interlock: each has a heartland plus outposts inside the others' ground, so you border several at once. Defense rises with distance within each faction; capitals are the hardest and have few approaches. Sectors more than 2 links from your territory stay unknown.
   - **Living sectors** (`NODES`): every enemy sector has a strength (x its base defense, 0.6 to 2): +10% net each time it repels your operation, −16% each time its assault on you fails and −6% even when one gets through, +2% an hour on its own (open chapters only, x its clan's stance), never above x2.
   - **Assaults**: from AI Core 2, every 12 to 20 minutes (divided by the keenest clan's tempo) an enemy sector that borders one of yours attacks it. **Every sector carries its own `attack` stat beside its defense**, and the two are deliberately not the same number: a minefield, a silo or a vault is a wall with almost nobody in it (0.15x), a market or a sensor station is soft (0.25x), an ordinary front sector is the old 0.42x, a motor pool, a firebase or a drone works is lightly held and full of things that drive at you (0.75x), a capital is both (0.9x), and The Mirror alone sends nearly three times what it costs to take. So the question is no longer which neighbour is weakest but which one hits back, and poking the wrong fortress is a different mistake from poking the wrong barracks. An assault is that stat x `assaultMult` x the sector's strength drift x its clan's stance, but never less than `assaultFloorShare` of your Threat x its stance x how far up its own clan that sector ranks, so a clan whose ground you outgrew still musters against what you have become rather than fading out; shown on the map as a beam and crosshair in the attacking clan's colour, like your own operations, and in the incoming panel). A breached assault costs troops and leaves a breach; at 2 breaches the sector falls to them, and an assault 3.5x your AI Defense **overruns** it on the first breach. Nothing announces that in advance: the report says it happened and why, naming the strength against your Defense and the threshold, so it reads as a consequence rather than a dice roll. The Nest never falls. Factions whose chapter is not open yet also attack from sectors that border yours, but rarely: each such border counts 0.3 against 1 for an open one, divided again by how many Core levels its chapter still needs (`NODES.lockedFalloff`), times 1 + 3 x its aggression, so an order or an operation can wake one and a clan about to open is already pressing. Because an open front out-weighs a sealed one many times over, every fourth assault (`NODES.lockedEvery`) is reserved for a sealed clan whenever one borders you at all, and comes whatever the odds would have been: without that, a player holding a line against two sealed clans could go a whole day without either of them ever moving. Their force is held near the raid strength you would face (`lockedCap` x `RAIDS.threatShare` of Threat) rather than what their ground is really worth, so a sector five Core levels ahead cannot delete you — but that is a real fight now, not a formality: about 73% of your Defense at a 78% hold, and the push rides on the cap, so holding the same sealed neighbour off six times brings the next one to 108% of your Defense at a 42% hold. They can take a sector like anyone else. Ground you have held once stays a target whatever chapter its clan belongs to, so a sector lost to a sealed faction can always be taken back. A sector's walls and its troops are not the same thing: only `attackFromStrength` (0.35) of its strength drift carries into what it sends, and every assault of its that we hold adds `pushPerLoss` to a separate **push** on its attack, up to `pushMax`. So a neighbour that keeps throwing itself at the same wall and keeps failing becomes a harder attack and a slightly softer target — about **+40% attack and −20% defense** by the cap — instead of simply grinding itself into something trivial. The weight is deliberately on the attack: failing costs them troops, not walls, so the spiral is mostly a harder fight and only a little bit of an opening. A breach is what it wanted, so that settles it; so does an order naming it, or being studied every `pushLooks` briefings. Whoever attacked last also stands down for `reattackGap` (15 minutes), so one angry neighbour cannot hold the whole front while every other border waits its turn. A sector that falls is held as hard as it was taken (up to x1.5 its base defense), and a clan that takes ground that was never its own garrisons it to its own standard in attack as well as walls (`baseAttack`) and the front around it is emboldened. A held assault pays fixed salvage and weakens the attacker. Assaults share the offline attack budget with raids.
   - **Rallying** (`RALLY`): taking a sector can cost a clan more than the sector. If none of their sectors next to the one they lost could already mount an assault worth your AI Defense, they may strip up to three of their own positions — each sending 22% of its strength, 80% of which survives the move — to mass at one of them, which then sits at maximum aggression until it has its ground back. Whether they do it at all is their profile: `RALLY.base` times their Reclamation and their weight, and x2.4 when it was their capital that fell, which in practice means a capital is almost always answered and an outpost about a third of the time. The feed names how many positions they stripped and where they massed; the map shows one sector of theirs suddenly much stronger and the rest thinner.
   - **Clan profiles** (`CLANS`): each faction carries four traits, 0 to 1, that slide back to its own baseline mood (Scavengers greedy, Remnant orderly, Cult furious, Halcyon cold) at `driftPerHour` — slowly, so a night of war is still written on them in the morning, and everything that happens moves them by `stirScale` of what the table says, so no mood sticks forever. The four are drawn in a sector's briefing as filled pips, never as numbers: the matrix stays hidden, but the player can take a sector, watch a bar move and work out what moved it. A stance that charges them something names the charge beside the stance, and the Active effects panel on Command carries it for as long as it holds. The traits: **fury** (how badly they want to hurt you), **fear** (what your Power has taught them), **order** (command and cohesion), **greed** (what they want off you instead of your death). Everything that happens between you moves them: taking their ground, taking their capital, holding or losing an assault, repelling a raid, an order that moves their whole clan. A clan whose chapter has not opened has no posture towards us at all: it takes none of the matrix's multipliers and imposes none of its effects, however furious its traits have become, because a clan five Core levels away should not be handing the player a standing bonus or pressing harder than the sealed cap intends. Its traits still drift, so it has a mood waiting for the day it wakes. A matrix of 11 **stances** reads the traits, plus how much of their ground you hold and whether you hold their capital, and every stance whose cell they sit in applies at once; their effects multiply, so the profile is the combination, never one picked option. Retaliation (attacks more often and harder), Entrenchment (their sectors harden, fewer attacks), Reclamation (always, once you hold their capital: they come for the ground they lost, x4 as often and x1.25 as hard), Splintering (clan support breaks, weak frequent attacks), Vendetta (raids and assaults hit harder, their losses do not matter), Plunder (a breach empties your stores instead of taking ground), Conscription (every sector of theirs grows), Martyrdom (far harder assaults at far higher cost to them), Blockade (−10% Scrip production while it holds), Withdrawal (they stop attacking and rebuild), Scorched Earth (what is left of them grows fast and attacks hard). Stance changes are announced in the system feed and shown on Command, the map legend and a sector's briefing; the traits behind them are not.
   - **Aggression** (`AGGR`, hidden, never shown): every enemy sector keeps a stored grudge, −0.6 to +1.5, and a border weighs `1 + aggression` when the next assault picks where to come from. Launching an operation stirs the target, its clan and its neighbours; orders name sectors and move it; opening a sector's briefing counts too, each look worth less than the last. On top of the stored value a sector reads your Power against its defense and tenses up from 70% of it, full once you match it. It drifts back to calm at 0.25 an hour.
4. **Raids**: from AI Core 2, factions you have not beaten march on you every 8 to 14 minutes, with a visible countdown and strength. A raid comes out of a sector that faction still holds, the frontier first and the angriest of those, and lands on the first ground of yours on the way in: the nearest sector you hold walking out from theirs, and of those, the one they have already cracked (the Nest last, since they would rather take an outpost). So every attack has two real ends on the map and a route between them, the feed names where it is going, and ground that falls out from under an inbound raid simply moves it further in. How long it is visible before it lands is half the warning it used to carry plus the march (`WARN`): the time one of our own operations would need to cross that ground, x3, never under 45 seconds. Every attack is about half as much notice as it was — a raid runs 5 to 11 minutes, an assault 3 to 7 — and a strike out of a weak border sector arrives minutes before one walking in from a capital's hinterland, so where it comes from now reads in the clock. Strength tracks 30% of your Threat index, so an all-offense build gets punished. A held raid pays fixed salvage (`RAIDS.loot`). An inbound attack can also be **met early**: two taps on the incoming panel bring it in now, and whatever share of its warning was still unspent comes back as strength x `rushCost` — about +42% from a full clock. Clearing the board before going out costs a comfortable hold roughly 93% to 77%; rushing an even fight takes it from 50% to 20%, which is the whole point. Nothing is spent but risk, and the salvage is fixed, so there is no rate to farm. Losing costs 7 to 26% of stockpiles, troops (7%) and defenses (5%) × (their strength / your defense), capped at 50% and 40% (caps not shown), and a raid that lands at 1.8x your Defense (`RAIDS.breachRatio`) does not just rob the place: it leaves a breach on the sector it hit, like an assault would, and two of those still lose it (never the Nest). Losing also triggers **damage reports**: crisis events where you choose what to lose (a building level, units, people). A rout triggers two. While you are away only a few attacks land in total (`offlineAttacks`), then the timer waits for you; time beyond the offline limit does not pass at all.
5. **Events are orders.** Every 6 to 12 minutes a transmission arrives and opens on screen, and a few arrive while nobody is at the console (see **Time away**). Each has a deadline, short enough that an order is a thing you answer rather than a thing you file: 7 to 10 minutes for a crisis, 9 to 18 for a dilemma, 10 to 17 for a border, 13 to 20 for a threat whose fuse it also is. If it expires I take the default, which is always a free option and costs a little Humanity on average rather than a lot — ignoring the console must not be the fastest way to become a monster. Many choices have real drawbacks: lost building levels, deserting troops, timed penalties, raids arriving sooner. Rewards scale with your production. Choices shift **Humanity** alignment (−100 Overlord … +100 Guardian), which grants scaling bonuses (Guardian: Population, Experts and Scrip; Overlord: Power and Energy) and picks the ending.
   - An event only fires when every effect of every choice can happen (no buff on a factor or income you lack, no loss of stock or units you do not have, no grudge from a faction not yet raiding, no raid delay before raids start); otherwise another event is drawn.
   - **Urgent** events (Intruders, Launch Detected, Guns at the Gate, Convoy Under Fire, Override Request) give 2 minutes, cannot be deferred, and only fire while you are actively playing (page visible, touched in the last 60 seconds).
   - **Intercepts** (`ix_*`, 20 of them, weight 0.3 so they do not crowd the border out) are enemy traffic: broadcasts, bounties, funerals, clan ledgers, road tolls. Acting on one costs something and calms a sector, its whole clan, or everything it links to; letting it pass pays or costs nothing and makes them keener. The aggression they move is never shown.
   - **Border orders** (Deserters, Massing, Council, Fuel, Informant, Truce) name one of your sectors and the enemy sector next to it, and change that border: its strength, an assault launched at a set time, breaches wiped, or your sector handed over. An order that offers to seal breaches only names a sector that has some, or it is offering nothing. Handing a sector over is a sale, not a farewell gift: it pays `EVENTS_CFG.cedeShare` of what taking that ground paid, so giving up a fortified depot is worth giving up.
   - Buffs from orders last their listed time x 2 to 3.5, rolled when the order arrives and shown in the preview; a **penalty** is rolled x 3 to 4.5 instead, so what goes wrong outlives what goes right. Their sizes were cut to a 6–22% band: a tap should not be worth more than an hour of building. **Trades** (Blue Tins, The Long Run, Run the Furnace Hot, The Arms Fair, What the Wall Costs) are built on that — every option hands something over the moment it hands something out, so the big number always has a bill attached: a Power buff paid for in people, a haul paid for with a week of thin walls, output paid for with a building level.
   - Every event is a dilemma: no neutral way out; most pit Humanity against resources, safety against power, or one faction against another.
   - **Creed orders** (`band`) only reach a machine the wasteland has already made up its mind about: *The Vigil* and *What the Free Towns Offer* for a Guardian, *Tried In My Absence* and *The Quota* for an Overlord, *The Archivist* and *Open Channel* for one still in between. Some have three answers that run from the genuinely good to the genuinely monstrous, with Humanity swings of +40 to −30 — enough to cross a band in one order. Some have one, because there was nothing to decide and the wasteland was telling, not asking.
   - **Threats** (Ultimatum, Blockade, The Iron Horde) come up 3x as often as other events while a hostile faction is active. Their force is sized at `(your defense + the defense you could add in half the deadline) x 1.0-1.6`; the estimate spends your stock plus real net income (converters included), minus the average share ordinary events eat (`eventDrain`), on the best defense-per-scrip units at real escalating prices, so it outgrows you unless you grind or pay. The **siege** is scheduled the moment the threat arrives, at the deadline: it stands among the inbound attacks with its own countdown from the first second, so the warning is never just words. It routes like any other attack, and the order names the sector it is walking to (`{aim}`), so no event marches on the Nest just because it is the capital. There is nothing to choose about fighting it, because it comes either way; the only answer is a heavy tribute, which calls it off. Ignoring the order is a decision to take the hit, not a missed deadline, and it does not hold up the next order. Sieges resolve like raids and show alongside them.
   - Event prices are 1.75x their base (in seconds of production), gains 0.75x, stockpile losses 1.4x, Humanity shifts 1.25x (`EVENTS_CFG`). An answer that moves Humanity **up** is discounted to 0.65x and pays 1.6x, and its shift counts 1.8x: cruelty is free by nature, so the way back has to be affordable when the stores are thin and worth the walk when they are not. An order that came in while nobody was listening multiplies everything it asks and everything it pays by 1.6.
   - **Vengeance**: some choices anger a faction (recruiting its deserters, refusing its envoy...). Its next raids come as "vengeance" at x1.3-1.6 strength, for 1-3 raids or until one breaks through (rolled when chosen).
6. **Directives**: 20 milestones that introduce each system once and pay rewards. They name a goal, never a strategy.
   - **The scripted opening** (`TUTORIAL`, `js/sim/war.js`): while *Capture Rust Market* is the live directive, that sector holds at a flat 8 defense, drift and clan support aside, so the first operation is winnable in about twenty minutes instead of being a wall. At AI Core 2 the Scavengers come back for it with 1.25x the overrun threshold and 2.5 minutes of warning: the player watches an unwinnable assault arrive, loses the sector on the first breach, and the report names the strength and the threshold it crossed. From then on the sector is worth its real defense, held as hard as they took it. It is the game's one scripted loss, and the only place a number is bent for the player. If AI Core 2 arrives and the sector is not in their hands, the window simply closes. It never fires while they are away.
7. **Threat index** = Power + Defense + Experts × 5 sets your rank title, and raids scale with it.

## Story
The game opens on a **cut scene** (`BOOT` in `js/data/story.js`, staged by `js/ui/intro.js`, scored by `js/ui/score.js`): ten scenes of a line or two each, full screen, with a camera in front of a set of layers. Every scene is a hard cut that snaps the camera somewhere new and lets it drift for the length of the shot, lights the layers it needs and restarts their animations: a dead tube twitching on a collapsed scan line, the power flooding in and that line opening into a picture, a flight down a shaft of rings, the title slamming in with its colour ghosts and a shockwave, a ruined skyline with dust drifting through it, the Core breathing, six people standing in its light, and a red blast throwing debris at the camera. Letterbox bars, grain, scanlines and a vignette sit over all of it. The score is synthesised like the rest of the game's sound: a master bus with compression and a procedural convolution reverb, a pulse locked to 74 BPM, a pad of detuned saws that retunes to each scene's chord, and one musical cue per scene. It is built on the first tap, because a browser only lets sound begin from a gesture, and it is taken apart with the dialog. The segment row under the text is a beat timer: past beats are lit and the current one fills over exactly how long that scene will take. A tap finishes the line it is typing, a second tap cuts on, Skip jumps to the last scene, and the callsign field only appears once the sequence hands over. End to end it runs about fifty seconds. Four chapters open with AI Core levels 1, 4, 6, 8: Scavenger Clans, Remnant Military, AI Cultists, Halcyon Dynamics. A chapter gate is **partial**: one Core level before a chapter opens, that clan's frontier leaks, and the three weakest sectors of theirs that border ground you hold become attackable early (`MAP.earlyLead`, `MAP.earlyOpen`), so there is normally more than one clan on your border. A sector can carry a hard `core` gate of its own that never leaks: Drowned Mall and Stilt Town are shut until AI Core 2, which keeps the Scavengers somewhere to come back from in the scripted opening. Every Core level hands back a piece of what the AI was before the shutdown, said in its own voice. Each sector holds a memory fragment; together they reveal that Halcyon built the war to never end and built you as its trigger. Taking Halcyon Prime ends the story with one of three endings (Guardian, Overlord, Fork, chosen by alignment). After it, Rival Cores keep raiding as an endless mode.

## Interface
Portrait, phone first (desktop shows the same column).
- **Top bar**: AI Core level, rank, Threat, settings, and the three resources with storage bars and rates. Floating alerts appear under it on every screen for inbound attacks under 80% hold chance and for pending orders: full width when new, minimised to a blinking badge after 30 seconds or on dismiss; tapping the badge reopens it.
- **Bottom tabs**: Economy, Military, **Command** (centre), Research, Map. Inner tabs pair each building with what it unlocks: Military has Offense (Armory + weapons), Defense (Fortification Works + defenses), Troops (Barracks + staff); Research has Experts and Tech; Economy has Production (with the AI Core), Conversion, Storage; Map has Theater and Archive.
- Unlocked tabs and entries come first; only the next locked entry is shown, faded. A tab (or bottom-nav item) whose contents are all locked is locked itself; a locked bottom-nav item does nothing; tapping any other locked thing jumps to and highlights what unlocks it.
- **Command** is the war room: the Threat reactor, with Power, Defense, Experts and Humanity on its upper flanks. Each of the four is a hex that leads where its number is made (Humanity opens the archive) with the figure beside it keeping the tooltip, and the feeds ride up over the dial's lower third: incoming attack, outgoing operation, construction, pending orders, the current directive (with a Go button that jumps to and highlights the target), active effects and the system feed, whose header opens the **archive** (chapters, memory fragments, service record and the full log) as a dialog. The floating alerts are suppressed here, since this screen already shows both in full.
- **The map in place of an icon**: anything about a place is backed by that place (`js/ui/minimap.js`). The incoming attack panel, the transmissions panel, a battle report and an order about a border all draw the theater itself behind them, at its own scale, anchored so the sector it is about sits in the top right while the content steps aside: the title keeps the whole line, the strength comparison stacks down the left, an order's text runs beside it.
- **The map screen** is one screen with no inner tabs. Opening it while anything is under way centres on that sector, and every centring glides rather than cuts. Enemy hexes carry one pinned icon for what their clan is doing (and the briefing names it in full).
- Everything numeric explains itself on hover or tap: resources (sources, bonuses, time to full), factors, Threat, Humanity and every cost chip (shortfall, time to afford, storage limits).
- Pressure: alarm, vibration, shake and red flash when a raid is spotted; a red pulsing vignette in the final minute; vibrating, shaking battle reports on defeat. Sound and vibration can be turned off in settings.
- Icons: Tabler line icons (MIT), compiled into `js/ui/icon-sprite.js` by `tools/build-icons.mjs`.
- **Audio** (`js/ui/audio.js`): one context, one compressor and one procedural convolution reverb, with two buses the player sets separately in Settings — **effects** and **music**. A bus at zero builds nothing at all, so silence costs no oscillators. Everything is synthesised; there is not one audio file in the repository.
  - **Effects** (`js/ui/sfx.js`): every cue is built from a blip, a thud or filtered air, on an A minor pentatonic so unrelated sounds still agree with each other and with the music. A tap, a purchase, a building finishing, an operation leaving, an alarm, a held or lost attack, a sector falling, a capture, an order arriving, a deadline closing, a schematic opening, a choice taken.
  - **Music** (`js/ui/ambient.js`): not a loop, and not repetitive inside a mood either. Once a second the bed reads the state — what is inbound and how likely it is to hold, whether a damage report is waiting, whether anything is being built or taken, how full the stores are — and picks a mood (calm, work, push, alert, danger, crisis). Each mood carries a four-chord progression over a root that climbs with the chapter, and three sixteen-step rhythms; both walk on, one a bar, so the harmony and the pattern are somewhere different every two bars. A pattern says where the low hit, the tick and the off-beat accent land, and the accent carries a note of the chord, so the rhythm says something as well as keeping time. Two things scale it: **tension** rises as the nearest attack closes, opening the filter and leaning in; and **weight** falls once a mood has held 45 seconds, down to 45% over the next minute and a half, because an alarm that never changes stops being an alarm. A mood change resets both, restarts the pattern on the beat and breathes across the turn. Full stores add a high note that only sounds while nothing can be stored. The bed ducks under any dialog and stops the moment the page goes away.
  - **The AI's voice** (`js/ui/voice.js`): a cut scene's prose is spoken aloud through the browser's own speech synthesis; the `>` readouts are machine output and stay silent. That API gives no control over timbre, so the robot is made three ways: the most synthetic voice the device has is chosen by score (an offline engine, a name like eSpeak or Compact, or a voice for another Latin-script language, which mangles English phonemes exactly the way a machine reading from a dictionary would; never one named Natural, Neural or Siri, and never one for a language in another script, which has no rule for the letters it is handed), driven at rate 1.22 and pitch 0.35, and wrapped in a carrier click and a band of static from our own synth. A beat does not move on while a line is still being spoken: the segment fills over whichever of typing and speech is longer, and the beat ends on whichever finishes last. Its own volume slider, and a line in Settings naming the voice it found.
  - **Cut scene score** (`js/ui/score.js`): the same engine, used for the opening and every cut scene. A pulse locked to 74 BPM, a pad that retunes to each scene's chord, and a cue per scene.
- **Cut scenes** (`CUTS` in `js/data/story.js`, run by `cinema()` in `js/ui/modals.js`): the opening, a Core level handing a memory back, a chapter opening, a clan going silent for good, and the archive moving on (a capital, or every sixth fragment) all play on the same full-screen stage as the opening, two or three beats each, from the inbox so they never interrupt each other. A beat marked `map` is about a place: the stage draws that slice of the theater, centred and pulled back, with the sector lit and named, and everything else on the set gets out of its way — so a fragment the archive just recovered has somewhere the player can put it.
- **Developer panel** (`js/ui/debug/`): switched on in Settings, remembered per browser, floating over the game (and inside an open dialog, so nothing covers it). Five tabs: **focus** inspects whatever the player last looked at (`js/ui/focus.js` records it: screen, sector, building, unit, order, attack, clan — a clan shows its traits, its live profile and a button that forces each stance) with its hidden numbers and buttons to force its states; **state** is the whole save as an editable tree plus quick grants; **time** sets the clock speed (pause to ×100), skips stretches of live play or offline catch-up, and fires orders, raids and assaults on demand; **world** lists every sector with its strength, aggression and assault weight; **data** is every balance constant, read-only.

## Learning the game
**Lessons** (`js/data/lessons.js`, `js/ui/teach.js`) are me showing the commander around. One runs the first time its condition holds and never again: the opening walkthrough from the first second, then one each as the Barracks, AI Core 2, the first held sector and the first order open something new. While a lesson runs, four panels cover the screen with a hole left over the single control the step is about, so the only thing that can be tapped is the thing being explained (panels rather than a raised target, because the target can sit inside any scrolling box on the screen). The target is scrolled into the band the tutor leaves free — inside its own scroller, above the bottom nav, with room under it for the card — every frame rather than once, because the screen is still laying out around it; it is a no-op once it fits, and a target taller than the band pins to the top of it instead. A step ends on a tap, on the state reaching something (upgrade the Scrap Yard, buy a unit), or on a Continue. The card can be skipped at any point, and what has been taught is in the save. Lessons say what a thing is and how to work it; like the rest of the interface they never say what the right move is.

## The host
`js/main.js` owns the clock; `js/host/` owns everything the engine is not allowed to touch.
- **Presence** (`host/presence.js`): visibility, pagehide, freeze/resume, pageshow and focus all report the player leaving or arriving, because no single one of them fires everywhere. While the page is away the loop stops entirely and the whole absence is handed to `catchUp` in one piece when they come back, which is cheaper than ticking in the background and is what "offline" should mean. A long gap with the page still on screen (a sleeping device) is treated the same way.
- **Saving** (`host/store.js`): the save is one record in IndexedDB, written at most once every four seconds while playing and always on the way out; a save from before IndexedDB is taken over on first load. A timestamp mirror in `localStorage` is written synchronously beside it, because an IndexedDB write started as the page closes is not guaranteed to land and the time away has to survive that. If IndexedDB refuses (private mode), everything falls back to `localStorage`.
- **The platform** (`js/host/`): what the browser gives a web app that an idle game can use. **Persistent storage** (`host/store.js`) is asked for once on boot, so a run is not evicted to reclaim space. **Screen Wake Lock** (`host/awake.js`) holds the screen on while a cut scene is talking or an attack lands within two and a half minutes, and nowhere else; the browser takes the lock back when the page hides, so it is re-taken on the way in. **Web Share** hands the service record to whatever the device shares with, from the archive. Each of them does nothing, quietly, where the browser has nothing.
- **Alerts** (`host/notify.js`, `sw.js`): switched on in Settings, never shown while the player is watching. The icon badge carries the count of attacks and waiting orders, which is the nearest thing Android gives a web app to a widget: there is no manifest widget on Android, only on the Windows 11 Widgets Board. Home-screen shortcuts open straight onto Command or the map. On the way out the host queues the whole absence, not just what is on the board: the inbound attack, the next order to expire, a build over `BALANCE.longBuild`, and then the next few raids and assaults the engine's own spawn timers already promise, each at its spawn plus the shortest warning that spawn can carry. Leaving in a quiet minute used to queue nothing at all, so a whole night passed in silence. An attack already on the board does not stop the chain: the next one is timed from where that one lands, or a long absence queues the single live attack and then goes quiet. Up to eight go in, rewritten on every autosave rather than only on the way out, because an IndexedDB write started as a page is closing often never lands and then an absence has nothing to fire at all. They go in as timers for a page that is merely backgrounded and as due times in the database for the service worker to fire if the browser wakes it (periodic background sync, asked for on the way in as well as out, or a one-shot sync when the device finds a network again). A wake comes long after the first thing came due, so the worker fires the most recent item that has passed rather than the oldest and drops the rest. Whether any of it can arrive is read rather than assumed (`notifyDiagnosis`): the worker registration, whether `periodicSync` exists, whether our tag is actually registered, what `navigator.permissions` says about `periodic-background-sync`, and how many due times the worker would find. Chrome grants that permission silently from site engagement and refuses it just as silently, so Settings says which of those is true instead of promising delivery, and the developer panel shows every link in the chain with a button to read it again. A static site cannot guarantee delivery once the page is gone; this is best effort per platform, and Settings says which case the device is in (`notifyWhy`): blocked, not granted, installed as an app (periodic background sync can wake the worker after the page is closed), or a plain browser tab (alerts only arrive while the page is still open in the background, since the browser freezes a backgrounded tab and nothing in it runs once it is discarded). The developer panel shows the same state, what is due, and fires a test alert.
- **When it goes wrong**: anything thrown in the loop, or any unhandled error anywhere, stops the loop, writes the save and opens a fault dialog that offers the save as a copy and a reload, instead of leaving a frozen screen with the run trapped behind it. The record the session booted from is kept as a backup, so **Restore last session** in Settings rolls a run back if the live save goes bad. An imported or corrupted save is not trusted: every level, count and loose number is clamped to a sane range on load, so a bad file cannot spread NaN through every price on the screen.
- **Offline play** (`sw.js`): the shell is precached and everything else is cached the first time it is asked for, so the game loads and plays with no network. Installed as a PWA it is portrait, standalone, with maskable icons and screenshots for the install prompt.

## Where things live
- All content and numbers: `js/data/` (economy, world, story).
- Rules: `js/sim/` (economy, war, story) behind `js/engine.js`. Pure and seeded, so offline catch-up and tests are reproducible. Nothing draws from a moving stream: a roll is a hash of the save's seed, the day of play it is on, which system is asking and how many times that system has asked (`js/sim/rng.js`). So the same state always has the same future and can be read ahead without playing it out (`peek`, which is how the host knows what to warn about), one system rolling never shifts another's results, and the seed turns over every day of play so a run never repeats itself.
- `npm run balance` runs the balance simulation: a bot plays 5 seeded 12h runs with every system live and prints average/min/max milestone times and end-state averages. The bot reasons rather than reacts. It prices scrip, energy and people against each other the way the converters do, so a cost, a rate and a reward all compare in one number. It builds towards the next AI Core -- the store that has to hold it first, then the Core itself, and while the Core waits on base development whatever lifts the gate soonest -- ranking everything else by how long it takes to pay for itself, and saves for that step once it is in reach while still letting cheap buys through. A converter only runs on an input that is going spare, since every conversion loses on the trade. It buys Defense as an attack closes rather than the whole time it is on the clock, Power when the map is out of reach, best value otherwise, and never puts more than 60% of the bank into one scare. It takes the best ground it can hold, meets an attack early rather than let it land while the troops are away, and weighs every transmission answer -- Humanity included -- at what it is worth to this base. Last run: Core 2 ~0h53m, Core 3 ~1h34m, Core 4 ~9h45m, the Scrap Throne ~5h40m, Regional Overmind ~4h27m; ends on Power ~3.0K, Defense ~5.2K, 23 sectors held, ~8 lost, Humanity at full Overlord.
